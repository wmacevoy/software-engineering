# SE1 quick reference

One line per principle of SE1, a guide of 104 software-engineering guidelines for humans and agents, condensed so a reader who will never open the chapters gets the move and its reason. These are guidelines, not law: they point, and you decide; where a line leaves a hole, that hole is a place to decide in. The ID finds the full text in `guide/` when a line is not enough.

## 1. Purpose

- **SE1/P1** Reliability, time, resources, and discomfort trade against each other; scope is the fifth and usually the honest one to move. A plan that does not say which is fixed, and which moves when wrong, is a wish with dates.
- **SE1/P2** Correct means it matches the spec; reliable means it works amid bad inputs and failing parts, and says so when it does not. The unseen failure turns a fault into an outage: when this is broken, how does anyone find out?
- **SE1/P3** Fear degrades judgment, and a frightened team drops exactly the practices that would have saved it. Discomfort shows weeks before the schedule does; someone going quiet is data.
- **SE1/P4** Backups, tests, CI, review each retire one specific fear; if you cannot name it, find it or stop. Worse than useless is one that quiets the fear without reducing the risk: a backup never restored, a test that cannot fail.
- **SE1/P5** The deliverable is the code plus a stranger's ability to run, reset, deploy, diagnose at 3am, and continue it in month seven. You are engineering the cost of the next change; write down why it is shaped this way while you know.

## 2. Unknowing

- **SE1/U1** K0 is known and true. K1, the known unknown, is cheap: ask or look it up. K2, the unknown unknown, costs a rewrite. K3, erroneous certainty, costs everything built on it, found last. K4, a falsehood chosen knowingly, is not on the ladder; it is a decision.
- **SE1/U2** Every practice moves something toward K0 before it charges full price: write K0 down; spike K1; expose K2 to reality early by integrating, demoing, asking a domain expert; meet K3 with evidence that could have said no; name each K4 and its blast radius.
- **SE1/U3** K3 costs nothing at the time: no search starts, because you think there is nothing to look for. Only reality and someone who does not share your assumption find it. Run it rather than reason; a guard never seen to refuse has not been seen to work. To show a flaw, show it failing rather than argue it.
- **SE1/U4** "The clock is right," "the token reflects the current role": nothing gets built without such falsehoods. Choose them deliberately; write where each is load-bearing and what breaks when it comes due. Chosen, an asset; held unknowingly, K3.
- **SE1/U5** "Two days" usually means "if nothing I haven't thought of happens." Mostly-K0 work gets a number, K1 a range, K2 only a time box. Say which you are handing over; when unsure, time-box a spike, then give a range.
- **SE1/U6** A constraint in one head is K3 for its holder, who thinks everyone knows, and K2 for everyone else, who cannot ask about what they do not know exists. Teammates leave; agents were never in the room. Put it in the repository.
- **SE1/U7** A K3 factory keeps saying "fine" with no way to say "wrong": an alert never seen firing, an unversioned citation, a status with no since, a default nobody chose, torn work left looking current. Give it a way to fail and watch it once; make it age visibly; make its source checkable.
- **SE1/U8** An agenda finds only what it looks for; K2 lives in what nobody asks about. Spend time with no task on purpose. For an agent: read the repository and what changed, and report what moved, tore, or went stale to FINDINGS or the queue, not code.
- **SE1/U9** A rule reads the same whether measured, decided, or guessed. Beside one you will rely on, say who claims it, since when, and what would show it wrong. Doubt the newest first; supersede a wrong one with its reason, never edit silently.
- **SE1/U10** "Is this usable?" never converts to K0; a measurement near it is a proxy, and "usability is 92" stops the question, which is K3. Convert what converts; for the rest name the judge and the occasion. A proxy may veto; only the judge may pass.

## 3. Feedback

- **SE1/F1** Where a test costs a million dollars, front-load analysis and sign-off; where it costs a second, loop. The cost is per decision, not per project: a route handler loops, a schema with real data under it gets care.
- **SE1/F2** The toolkit: pure functions with state at the edges, an injected clock, seeded randomness, faults on demand, one-command reset, conformance checked from outside, logs recording each decision's inputs. It multiplies against every future change. Hard to test is information about the design.
- **SE1/F3** Loops by cost: type checker, running it, unit, integration, review, client demo, production. When something slips through, ask which cheapest loop could have caught it and put a check there. "Wrong product" is caught only by a person at a demo.
- **SE1/F4** Nothing sags or cracks: a bad design holds itself up at full speed with the tests passing. Every signal a physical structure gives for free must be built on purpose. If this were wrong, what would tell us, and when?
- **SE1/F5** Aim at coverage and get tests with no assertions; at velocity and estimates inflate; at "every requirement has a test" and get tests that assert nothing. Keep the reason attached to the rule wherever it appears; ask how you would cheaply fake it.

## 4. Requirements

- **SE1/R1** "We need an app that shows which lots have spaces" is a pain plus a guess, arriving confident so it triggers no investigation. Walk back to the pain: what happens today when it goes wrong, who gets the call? The real user may be someone else.
- **SE1/R2** The client cannot say what happens when a sensor goes silent until they read "the count freezes and shows as current" and say no. Draft to be wrong cheaply: numbered, versioned, observable, with numbers, invented ones marked. "Responsive and up to date" gets nodded at.
- **SE1/R3** Nobody volunteers: every kind of person it touches, how many in two years, what we are not building, absent/late/wrong/lying inputs, the bad day, how long it may be down, what data about people it keeps, who is affected outside the room. Ask those that could invalidate the plan first.
- **SE1/R4** Every sentence in a requirements document is a purchase someone must authorize. To "we want everything," answer "yes, and here is what it moves."
- **SE1/R5** Could another team satisfy it differently, and can you check it from outside without the source? A table name, library, or status code in a requirement is design. Editing a requirement to match what the code does is a design change in costume.
- **SE1/R6** Per input decide: absent (wait, serve stale, refuse?), late (how long is too long?), wrong (reject, clamp, flag?), lying (200 OK, frozen since Tuesday: how would anyone notice?). Unstated, whatever the HTTP library does becomes the product. A feature an input announces that you do not support gets refuse, read-only, or read-write, never "ignore."
- **SE1/R7** Scale, speed, recovery, and availability: unwritten, the code answers them. Get the client's numbers with a horizon and do arithmetic on them. Recovery: what happens while it is down, and who, which command, how long to get back, real only if run and timed. Availability is a budget with period and beneficiary; unstated it is 100%, which forbids deploys.
- **SE1/R8** Security, law, and ethics are requirements on every project; their absence looks like presence until a breach or regulator sets the deadline, and the harm cannot be undone. Ask early someone who knows the rule. Write the threat model; decide what you keep about people and how long; the safest record is one never kept.
- **SE1/R9** Four lists, nearly free: scope decisions with reasons, non-goals, not yet answered, deferred. Each ends an argument permanently, and a week-four collision is found by grep rather than in review in week nine.
- **SE1/R10** Once someone depends on a statement, a change in meaning is a new version, not an edit; the diff is your best conversation with the client. Cite by the version that last changed it (REQ2/UI-1), so a change makes every stale citation findable by search.
- **SE1/R11** Story, criteria, an acceptance test written to fail today, then the observables that test needs: those fields and states are the API. A story with no test is a wish; a test with no story is accidental complexity. Name tests by versioned requirement ID; that is the trace.

## 5. Design

- **SE1/D1** Code is the description; what runs is state plus the rules that change it. State outlives its code, is shared, ages, and changes while observed. Difficulty is how much state a change can reach; list every piece: owner, copies, how stale, what the user sees meanwhile.
- **SE1/D2** A cache is any copy that might not match the original: a fetched variable, a count column, a login token carrying a role. Naming it one asks the right questions: how stale can it get, how would you know, what does the user see meanwhile?
- **SE1/D3** Once state lives in two places, each copy is locally correct and they disagree. Ask not how to sync them but how wrong each may be and who gets told. A message may not arrive, or arrive twice, or out of order; a returned call does not prove the write.
- **SE1/D4** Make doing it twice equal doing it once, so "did that go through?" stops mattering. "Set to 37" is idempotent; "one car arrived" is not: the caller supplies a key, and the same key twice is one operation. For ordering, carry a sequence number.
- **SE1/D5** Wall-clock says when; a monotonic counter says how long. Compute staleness from elapsed times only (sender's reported age plus receiver's hold); receipt time alone is not enough, since a frozen sender gets a fresh receipt on every poll. Keep wall-clock too, so a disagreement reveals a broken sender. Record past moments in UTC, future local times with zone; decide what a day is; test the boundaries.
- **SE1/D6** Anything computed cannot go stale: derive in one shared place rather than storing copies. Any value that exists twice needs a sentence saying which wins and that a mismatch is a fault. Put age in the data as a field. Record what a decision was based on.
- **SE1/D7** Ruling code touches the world: sensor, socket, database, clock, globals. Serving code takes values and returns values. Keep ruling to a few thin lines at the edges; put the logic in pure functions testable with plain numbers. Hard to test means a rule is tangled into the serve.
- **SE1/D8** Essential complexity is in the problem (sensors lie, clocks disagree); accidental is yours (the bundler, five layers of indirection). What requirement would have to disappear for this to go away? "None" means accidental, and it must justify its carry; yours is invisible to you because you understand it.
- **SE1/D9** People understand a system by guessing the next part from the last; once guessing stops working it is unmaintainable. One thing, one word; one word, one thing; a new noun is a decision. One person holds the shape and says no, even to good ideas; agents author, humans own it.
- **SE1/D10** Can you use it correctly without reading inside? If not, the boundary is a lie. Leaks: failure (a timeout behind "returns a number"), time (a read that is a round-trip), meaning (200 OK is not "current"), trust. A contract that does not say how it fails has said nothing.
- **SE1/D11** Renaming a variable is free; a format with data in it, a published URL, a distributed key are one-way doors. Once a second party depends on an interface it is a promise: changing a field's meaning is breaking even with the type unchanged, so version or rename.
- **SE1/D12** cost = build + carry × lifetime. Carry (every future reader, upgrade, CVE week) never shows on a schedule. A dependency is a permanent obligation; adding one is a proposal. Deletion is the highest-return work nobody schedules; ask each iteration. Data is heaviest; decide retention now.
- **SE1/D13** Evidence is "signed by a key in the registry, here is the check," not a feeling. A signature proves who said it and that it was unaltered, not that it is true. A human signature, "I read this and stand behind it," cannot be delegated.

## 6. Testing

- **SE1/T1** Configuration (missing at boot), status (broken now), input (absent, late, wrong, lying), output (impossible result), state (drifted into nonsense), time, space. If you cannot say which kind of fear a test retires, find it or delete the test, and watch it fail once.
- **SE1/T2** One pass/fail visible from across the room. Refuse to start if a precondition is missing, loudly: a missing secret is an exit code, not a dev default. Invariants checked in production, with a tested safe state. A mock per input that misbehaves on request. Time and space budgets asserted.
- **SE1/T3** Write the test, run it without the change, see red, then green. For code that already exists, break it on purpose and watch red. Same for sanitizers, coverage, and linters: each can be switched off silently, so make each fire once.
- **SE1/T4** Flaky tests train a team to ignore red, disarming the suite before the real failure. Unseeded randomness, the wall clock, shared mutable state, fixed ports are banned for that reason. Fix or quarantine the day it is found.
- **SE1/T5** Repeatability belongs to the state a test touches. Local (created and destroyed) is safe; sharded (its own slice) is safe; global and written is a race condition, not a test. Fix in order: make it local, shard it, serialize and say why. Inject the clock.
- **SE1/T6** A mock is a K4 about the real thing, false the day you write it. Make every mock misbehave on request (slow, down, stuck, wrong); run one conformance check against mock and real; integrate continuously, since a mock turns scheduling risk into integration risk.
- **SE1/T7** UI tests are global-state tests in disguise: shared server, real clock, animations. Pull the logic out and test it as functions; then the DOM in a headless browser; then a person. Assert on text and state, not pixels.
- **SE1/T8** Write the tests that would pass when it works; they say what the thing is and what it need not be. For each attack in the threat model, demonstrate it first; trust no defense until you have watched it refuse.
- **SE1/T9** You cannot fix a rumor, only change something and fail to observe the symptom, which feels like fixing. First move: reproduce, ideally as a failing test. If it will not, time-box, add logging of inputs at the suspected decision, write the open question in FINDINGS.
- **SE1/T10** Examples check the cases you imagined, the ones you were least likely to get wrong. Property tests state a rule for every input and shrink failures; fuzzing automates wrong and lying inputs; mutation testing flips the code and reports which tests never noticed.
- **SE1/T11** When a format or protocol is owned elsewhere, its implementation is the only judge. Write with yours, read with theirs, and back; every disagreement is your bug or a FINDINGS entry the spec never mentioned. Pin its version; do not read its source on clean-room work.

## 7. Operating

- **SE1/O1** An image tag is a family, not a file: it pulls different builds per architecture, and an unlocked install resolves differently next week. Pin digests per platform, lock dependencies, and choose: one platform, both, or "almost" with the load-bearing places written down.
- **SE1/O2** The manifest names the major.minor you are compatible with; the lock pins exact patch and hash; commit both; install with the command that refuses drift. Pin everything and never update and the software dies of neglect: schedule updates and let the suite follow each.
- **SE1/O3** CI runs the same command you run, with the gate in the script, so no second recipe drifts. Build and run on every platform you ship to. A convention CI does not enforce is not a policy; enforce what matters so review attends to what no tool sees.
- **SE1/O4** Logs explain decisions with their inputs, never holding secrets or personal data. Alerts on symptoms users feel, each naming a runbook; liveness and readiness separate. An alarm that fires when nothing is wrong is broken: tag injected faults, make maintenance a state, test each alert by injecting its fault.
- **SE1/O5** Every remote call has an explicit timeout shorter than the caller's deadline. Retries are bounded, jittered, only on idempotent operations, at one layer. Per dependency choose fail-fast or degrade, visibly. Never swallow an error: a catch that logs and carries on serves wrong answers quietly.
- **SE1/O6** A deploy is finished when the thing reports healthy and the system notices, not a person: health-gated, automatic revert, the rollback timed before the window opens. Revert works only if the old code can read the new data.
- **SE1/O7** During any deploy old and new run at once; for devices and cached browsers that is normal. Expand, then contract: add alongside, write both, read either, backfill, switch, then remove, each step undoable. Readers tolerate unknown fields and never change a field's meaning. Rehearse migrations, timed, on production-sized data.
- **SE1/O8** Least privilege per component and key. Secrets never in the repository, prompts, or logs; refer by name; a leaked one is rotated. Authorize every request on the server. A missing secret is an exit code, not a default. Demonstrate the threat model's attacks before trusting the defense.
- **SE1/O9** Mitigate before diagnosing: roll back, fail over, flag off, serve stale with its age. One leads, one communicates, status on a schedule. Runbooks are real only if run recently and timed. Afterward, write it up without blame in FINDINGS and change a test, alert, or runbook.
- **SE1/O10** Profile before optimizing; judge speed by the 95th or 99th percentile, not the average. But do run the load test at the peak the requirements state, on production-sized data, again when the data grows: an untested capacity requirement is a hope.
- **SE1/O11** A library, firmware, or file format is deployed by someone else, months later, and what it writes stays written after your fix. So each release gets a one-way door's care: a second reviewer, a checklist every time. Refuse unknown features rather than guess. Bug reports are your monitoring.

## 8. Work

- **SE1/W1** Requirements, design, develop, test, deploy, maintain: the order is right in every method. Methods differ only in how much goes through each step at once and whether the test comes at the end or inside every step.
- **SE1/W2** Where the one real test costs a million dollars, moving everything checkable before it is right; waterfall is a discipline that cannot afford to be wrong twice. With cheap tests it schedules K2's arrival for the last step, at full price. Royce called his own drawing risky.
- **SE1/W3** Each step tests itself: requirements by a client objecting to a precise claim; design by a contract, a misbehaving mock, a failing test; code by red then green; the iteration by a demo, the only instrument for "wrong product"; deploy by health gate and revert.
- **SE1/W4** A route handler: change it, run it, keep or revert. A schema with real data, a published API, a distributed key, data about people: a small waterfall inside the loop, design on paper, review by someone not sharing your assumption, done once.
- **SE1/W5** "Can't start B until A is done" is almost never a fact about the world; B does not know A's shape. An interface, a mock that misbehaves, and a test that fails today dissolve the arrow without A existing; then integrate continuously. Sequence around irreversibility, not incompleteness.
- **SE1/W6** A thin vertical slice, click to query to response, for a parking-count system: one lot, one sensor with its fault control, the collector, the page showing live, stale, and unknown: built first, then widened. Horizontal layers all finish at the same time, never. Carry the load-bearing idea in the first slice so it is tested under real weight.
- **SE1/W7** Fear rises sharply as a deadline nears, exactly when it produces skipped tests, bundled diffs, unreviewed merges. Change the conditions, not the willpower: make deploying routine and reversible, cross the gap early, put real fences along the way (a demo to an actual client, a check that exits nonzero; a status meeting proves nothing), and keep slack; a plan at 95% utilization cannot absorb a guaranteed K2.
- **SE1/W8** Falling down the waterfall is reaching design and code before the requirements. Tells that you are falling, before the client has objected to a numbered requirement: a schema or framework chosen, a "temporary" column, "privacy later," no non-goals. The railing back up: expensive questions first, answers written down, assumptions read back, then descend on purpose as a time-boxed spike with one question.
- **SE1/W9** One to two weeks; when the period ends, scope moves, not the date. Not done without all four: a running thing from a clean reset, a diff to the requirements, a FINDINGS entry, a QUEUE with a done sentence per item. Then: what can we delete?
- **SE1/W10** REQUIREMENTS_vX, DESIGN_vX, QUEUE, FINDINGS: in the repository, versioned with the code, not in a wiki or chat. QUEUE is the shared cursor so two workers do not take one task; FINDINGS is the ledger only reality writes.
- **SE1/W11** Done is the whole checklist: a versioned requirement ID named, or chore; a test naming that ID, seen failing; clean checkout builds and full suite passes; docs it made untrue updated in the same change; one concern, with omissions stated; the decisions it must not break listed by ID, since reviewers rarely catch an unnamed one; reviewed by someone else; signed by a person who read all of it.
- **SE1/W12** Rebuilding a working set from code invents reasons. Write what the code observably does as requirements marked (inferred) until someone who can decide confirms; design from structure, reasons with a source or (inferred); FINDINGS from reverts and hotfixes; QUEUE from untested requirements; read it back.
- **SE1/W13** A finding retires "we already learned this once" only if it changes something that outlives the people who learned it: a rule, a test, a check that refuses the change. End every finding with what it changed, or why nothing.
- **SE1/W14** mission < fundamental shape < requirements < design < develop: moving a lower one can move everything above. Move it on purpose, by its owner, in a new version, then walk up and see what no longer holds. Code that quietly disagrees with its design has drifted, not moved it.

## 9. People

- **SE1/H1** A system copies the communication structure of the team that built it, so choose the team's shape on purpose. Coordination grows with pairs, not heads, so adding people to a late project makes it later; the fix is a smaller written interface, not a meeting.
- **SE1/H2** Absent: nothing known to one head. Late: steps small enough that late shows in days. Wrong: review by someone not sharing the assumption. Lying: "basically done" is a sensor stuck at 200 OK; check the age of the last thing that actually ran.
- **SE1/H3** Architect holds the glossary and design and says no; Liaison is one voice to the client; Planner keeps the QUEUE with a done sentence per item; Toolsmith owns the cost of a test. Rotate each iteration: the hand-off reveals what lived in one head.
- **SE1/H4** Before splitting work: name the seam, write its contract including absent/late/wrong/lying, write the mock that misbehaves, write the test that fails today, integrate daily. A meeting that keeps recurring on one topic means an interface is missing. Stand-up: done, next, blocked, since when, a discomfort if noticed.
- **SE1/H5** The client knows the domain, pain, and money, and cannot tell you what happens in a case they never faced, or that their solution is a guess. They size a requirement by spaces and dollars; you size it by invalidated assumptions; the two are unrelated. Surprised: you failed; find the delay.
- **SE1/H6** Every scope decision and K4 is a sentence the team believes and the client was never asked. Read each back as a question: "We're assuming nobody has a claim on a space before their car is in it. True?" Most take two seconds; one gets "well, except…"
- **SE1/H7** The first client conversation: ninety minutes in week one, at the site: the pain (who gets the call), the census of every kind of person touched (the one teams skip), the bad day, the numbers with horizons, the edges, the assumptions read back. REQUIREMENTS_v1 within forty-eight hours: "tell us what is wrong with it."
- **SE1/H8** A demo is a test, so run it like one: the real system from the one-command reset; the client drives while you watch their hands; write every "huh" and "can it…" verbatim and cost them afterward; break something in front of them; read non-goals aloud; thirty minutes.
- **SE1/H9** A slip found in week two is planning; in week eleven it is a crisis. Within a day of knowing, to the person it affects, with a number and an option: drop the operator screen, or slip the demo a week. "I'm stuck" on day one costs a day.
- **SE1/H10** First, is it a category dispute (what requirement would have to disappear)? Then intensity by reversibility: a one-way door gets the whole team and a written decision; otherwise the role holder decides, with reason and revisit date. Still split and cheap: spike both. Reopening needs new information, not a mood.
- **SE1/H11** Someone behind is a queue near saturation: each new item waits longer than the last. Do not add work to the person who knows most. Redistribute, pair, or cut scope, in that order, with since when. Treat it as a character flaw and it recurs silently.
- **SE1/H12** Nobody is blamed for K2. A finding says "findable by X; X is now on the list," not who should have asked. A client who feels blamed stops volunteering the next surprise. To "I should have mentioned it": "You did. We didn't ask."

## 10. Agents

- **SE1/A1** Producing code got cheap much faster than knowing it is right, so everything scarce moved to specification, verification, review, and ownership. The bottleneck is attention, not typing. Now that writing this is cheap, what is the expensive part?
- **SE1/A2** Fluent, confident, plausible, occasionally wrong is the exact shape of K3, arriving faster than anyone checks. "This should work" is K3 by construction: run the check, and the human runs it again before signing. Give the agent one command to run and one to reset.
- **SE1/A3** Every session starts from the repository: a constraint said only in chat, or in a private agent memory, does not exist next session, and the agent hands back a clean, tested violation of it. What the client says is written down before anything is built on it; the contract precedes the code.
- **SE1/A4** "How does a competent stranger find the five files that matter in ten minutes?" is a repository property: component boundaries, a README per component, grep-able IDs. Doc-comments restating signatures, huge files, and commented-out code cost context, and stale prose reads like true prose. Comment only where the code cannot say why.
- **SE1/A5** Spend the saved time on fixtures, harnesses, seed data, fault scripts, conformance checks, the boring second and third test case, and the simple version a newcomer can read. That multiplies against every future change; more features, and abstractions nobody asked for, do not.
- **SE1/A6** A diff you cannot review is a diff you cannot sign. Ask for the smallest change that does the job, one concern, nothing bundled that was not asked for. Make rejection cheap.
- **SE1/A7** Whoever signs owns it. Read the whole diff, not the summary: the skipped line added a dependency or asserted nothing. A Co-Authored-By trailer credits the agent; the signature says "I read this and stand behind it." The agent does not commit, push, or publish unless asked.
- **SE1/A8** Agents are fastest at the bottom (code, tests, migrations) and at accidental complexity; at the top, where the work is conversation, law, and proportion, they search and draft but cannot decide. Humans own the structure; agents author and advise. Cost a scope collision before the prompt.
- **SE1/A9** Hand it: the second and third test case, fixtures, fault scripts, "which assumptions does this sentence invalidate?", a migration from a written design, boilerplate. Keep: the first test, the requirement the harness checks, the decision about the answer, the schema and whether keeping the data is legal, the vocabulary, the retro.
- **SE1/A10** Ask the model you are using: what did you read that you should not have needed, what would have let you get this right the first time, what did you assume because nobody wrote it down? Put the answers in the repository, as proposals from something with no stake.
- **SE1/A11** A compaction summary or handoff note is recalled, not observed, and the next context cannot tell by feel; in trials a wrong one sent every agent into a frozen change. What worked: a rule loaded at every start, or a check that ran on the work unasked. Where summary and source disagree, the source wins.
- **SE1/A12** There is no end of things an agent can find to do, each costing attention. Unless directed otherwise, prefer not to spend it; when you do, spend it reducing attention needed later. Aim for the done sentence met. If nothing would change because of this, why is it still open?
